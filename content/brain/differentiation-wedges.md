# Differentiation Wedges for Occupied Markets

## Why a wedge beats a head-on clone

Entering a market with three or more funded competitors requires a wedge: a narrow line of attack the incumbent is structurally unable or unwilling to copy. A broad clone loses on distribution, integrations, and switching costs before product quality ever matters. The test for a real wedge: matching your move must cost the incumbent more than losing the segment, in revenue, roadmap complexity, or repositioning. When scoring competition, an idea with no identifiable wedge should score low regardless of pain severity, because pain the incumbent can address in one release cycle is not a durable opening. A wedge converts an occupied market from a negative signal into a validated one: someone already proved willingness to pay.

## Niche-down: own one audience

Take the horizontal tool and rebuild it natively for one vertical. The value is everything the generic workflow forces that vertical to bolt on: templates, workarounds, exported spreadsheets, compliance done by hand. Evidence the wedge is open: search the incumbent's community, support forum, and review pages for the vertical's name. Repeated threads asking how to adapt the product for that industry, answered with hacks rather than features, indicate unmet severity and existing willingness to pay. Viability heuristic: at least 5,000 reachable companies in the niche, and an incumbent roadmap that has ignored their requests for two or more years. Retention follows: vertical products churn less because they hold domain data, industry integrations, and compliance logic that a generic tool never will.

## Workflow-first: own one job end to end

Incumbents drift into suites, and any single job gets buried under navigation built for everything else. The wedge is picking one job-to-be-done and covering it completely, from intake to final output, including the steps users currently perform in spreadsheets and email around the tool. Evidence the wedge is open: the job spans three or more products held together with manual glue such as CSV exports, screenshots, and re-keyed data. Severity check: glue work recurring weekly and consuming an hour or more per person signals a budget line waiting to exist. The win condition is subtractive. You have the wedge when a customer can cancel two subscriptions after adopting you, not when they add a third.

## Price-model wedge: change how it is charged

This is not discounting. Competing on price level invites a race the incumbent's margins win. Competing on price structure exploits a misalignment between the incumbent's meter and how customers perceive value: flat pricing against per-seat, usage-based against forced annual seats, per-outcome against per-login. Evidence the wedge is open: review complaints about paying for inactive seats, bill-shock threads, procurement pushback on minimum commitments. Heuristic: when 20 percent or more of negative reviews target the pricing structure rather than the amount, the wedge exists. It is durable because following you means repricing an entire installed base, which incumbents defer for years.

## Audience wedge: serve the segment incumbents ignore

Incumbents chase larger contracts up-market, and a segment below or beside them gets priced out or complexity-locked out: solo operators, small agencies, non-profits, a specific geography, non-admin roles inside the buyer's company. Evidence the wedge is open: the incumbent removed its starter tier, raised minimum seat counts, or moved core features behind enterprise plans, followed by a visible spike in downgrade and cancellation threads. Track pricing-page history: a deleted cheap plan is an invitation. The wedge doubles as a channel advantage, because ignored segments cluster in searchable communities where acquisition cost stays low and word of mouth compounds.
