# The Offer Value Equation

## Value is a ratio, not a sum

A compelling offer runs on four levers. Two push value up: the size of the outcome the buyer dreams of, and the buyer's perceived likelihood that this specific product will deliver it. Two drag value down: the time delay before the first real result, and the effort and sacrifice the buyer must invest to get there. The two upside levers multiply each other, and their product is divided by the two friction levers. The arithmetic is unforgiving: a zero anywhere kills the whole offer. A massive promise with no credibility is worth nothing, and instant delivery of an outcome nobody wants is worth the same. When scoring a SaaS idea, rate each lever on its own evidence, then check the ratio. A strong dream outcome divided by a three-month integration collapses toward zero, and no onboarding polish rescues a numerator that was never there.

## Dream outcome: severity and willingness to pay

The dream outcome lever is read from problem severity, not from feature appeal. The strongest public signal is money already moving: buyers paying an agency, a contractor, or a stack of duct-taped tools to get a worse version of the result. A budget line that already exists means willingness to pay is proven and the idea only has to redirect it. Complaint language is the second signal. Posts that mention lost revenue, compliance penalties, missed deals, or hours burned every week raise the lever. Language like "annoying," "would be nice," or "minor pain" lowers it, because nice-to-have outcomes produce trials that never convert. Heuristic: if you cannot find people describing the problem in units of money or hours within a few searches, score the dream outcome low regardless of how elegant the solution sounds.

## Perceived likelihood: proof the outcome actually happens

Buyers do not purchase the outcome, they purchase their belief that the outcome will happen for them. For a validator, the category's track record is the evidence. Competitor reviews that say "it works but the interface is painful" raise this lever for the whole category: the mechanism is proven and the complaint is execution. Reviews that say "we never saw the promised result" lower it for everyone, because the market has learned to distrust the claim. A narrow wedge raises perceived likelihood cheaply: an offer aimed at one vertical, one workflow, or one channel lets the buyer see themselves in the promise. Heuristic: specific beats superior. "Cuts invoice disputes for freight brokers" is more believable than "AI-powered financial operations," even with identical technology behind it.

## Time delay: distance to the first result

Time to value predicts trial conversion and early churn better than feature depth. Read it from the setup the idea structurally requires: data imports, API integrations, approvals from another team, or a cold-start period before the product has enough data to be useful. Each dependency is a division. Heuristic: if the first meaningful result cannot arrive inside one session, expect trial abandonment; if it needs more than a week or another department's cooperation, expect the deal to stall entirely. Public evidence patterns that lower this lever: competitors advertising "implementation support," onboarding webinars, or dedicated success teams, all of which signal a slow path that a faster wedge can attack.

## Effort and sacrifice: what the buyer must give up

Sacrifice is the switching cost plus the behavior change. Rip-and-replace offers demand migration, retraining, and political risk, so they divide value hard. Offers that sit inside an existing workflow, such as a browser extension, a Slack app, or a layer over the current system of record, keep this lever near one. Read sacrifice from public evidence: reviews complaining about migration pain, forum threads asking "how do I export my data," and churn stories that blame setup rather than results all mark a category where effort is the open flank. Heuristic: augment first, replace later. The idea that asks for the smallest change in daily behavior wins the channel even against a stronger feature set.
