# Why Volume-Built SaaS Dies

Most shipped-fast SaaS products fail for one of five recurring reasons. Each pattern below has an observable evidence signature and a heuristic you can apply before writing code.

## Unfelt Problems Kill Products Before Launch

The most common failure is solving a problem the target user does not feel. Severity is not what the founder believes: it is what the user already does about the problem today. High severity leaves traces: current spend on an inferior alternative, a manual workaround that eats two or more hours per week, a hire whose job partly exists because the problem is unsolved, or dated complaints in niche communities. If none of these traces exist, the problem is an intellectual observation, not a felt pain, and no amount of execution fixes that. Demo enthusiasm and "I would definitely use this" are not demand signals. A prepayment, a deposit, or a scheduled onboarding call is. Heuristic: before building, name ten specific people who exhibit the workaround behavior today. If you cannot, severity is unproven and the build is premature.

## Single-Channel Dependence Is a Countdown Timer

A product whose signups come overwhelmingly from one channel it does not control is already dying, it just has not noticed. The evidence signature is a traffic graph with one launch-day spike followed by a flatline: the launch was the channel, and launches do not repeat. Heuristic: if more than 70 percent of new signups come from a single rented channel, treat distribution as unsolved regardless of revenue. A durable wedge pairs one repeatable acquisition channel with retention strong enough that usage compounds: referrals, content that ranks, integrations that surface the product inside someone else's workflow. Ask of any channel: can I do this again next month at similar cost, and does it improve with repetition? If the answer is no to either, it is an event, not a channel.

## Vitamin Pricing Means Willingness to Pay Never Materializes

Nice-to-have products monetize at hobby rates and churn at lethal ones. The diagnostic is churn: monthly logo churn above 8 to 10 percent says the product is a vitamin, whatever users claim in surveys. Painkillers show the opposite signature: annual plans chosen voluntarily, expansion revenue from existing accounts, and tolerance for price increases. Heuristic: if a 20 percent price increase would trigger a measurable churn spike, pricing power is absent and the monetization model is decorative. Willingness to pay is tested before building, not after: ask for money, a card, or a signed commitment, and treat anything short of that as a polite no.

## Feature Advantages Lose to Distribution Advantages

Competing on feature parity against an incumbent with owned distribution is a losing trade. The incumbent can ship your differentiating feature in a quarter and expose it to an installed base you would need years to reach. A feature is a real wedge only when it is bound to a niche the incumbent cannot serve without breaking its own pricing, positioning, or architecture. Evidence pattern to check: does your differentiation survive the incumbent bundling a free version of it? If the honest answer is no, the moat is a head start measured in months, and the competition score should reflect that.

## Token-Burn Economics: Building Got Cheap, Being Wrong Did Not

AI-assisted development collapsed the cost of producing software. It did not collapse the cost of producing the wrong software. Distribution effort, support load, and maintenance still price in months per product, so the bottleneck moved from engineering to validation. The failure mode is portfolio spam: shipping twelve unvalidated products a year and losing to a builder who ships three validated ones, because code generation parallelizes and distribution does not. Heuristic: validation spend must precede build spend. A weekend of demand testing, fake-door pages, waitlist-to-payment conversion, or ten problem interviews costs less than one percent of what a dead product costs in opportunity and attention. Cheap builds make discipline more valuable, not less.
