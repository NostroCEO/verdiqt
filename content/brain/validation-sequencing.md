# Cheapest Kill Shot First: Sequencing Validation Tests

## The test menu, priced in founder hours

Seven tests cover every dimension of a SaaS verdict, and each has a real cost in hours. Sequencing is a budget problem, so price the menu before touching it.

Wedge cut, 2 hours: cut scope on paper until you find the smallest slice one buyer would pay for this quarter. Stresses build cost.
Pricing probe, 3 hours: put a specific price in front of prospects, on a page or in ten direct messages, and ask for a commitment. Stresses willingness to pay.
Competitor teardown, 3 hours: read rivals' pricing pages, changelogs, and one-star reviews. Churn complaints in public reviews are a map of unmet severity. Stresses competition.
Fake door, 4 hours: a landing page with a signup button and a modest push of traffic. Stresses demand signals.
Forum interviews, 6 hours: find people already complaining in public threads and get six of them on a call. Stresses problem severity.
Channel test, 8 hours: run one acquisition channel for real, small ad spend or three posts in the community where buyers live. Stresses distribution.
Ship it, 0 hours: no additional test time, because the build itself becomes the test.

The full menu costs 26 hours. Most ideas deserve to die well before hour 26.

## Sort by cost, then promote the killer

Cheapest first is the default order, not the law. The true sort key is expected verdict change per hour. Before running any test, ask one question: if this comes back negative, do I still build? If the answer is yes, the test is theater, so skip it. The test worth running next is the one whose failure would most change the verdict, which usually means attacking the dimension you are most nervous about rather than the one easiest to confirm. A 3 hour teardown outranks a 4 hour fake door when a crowded market is the live objection: a red teardown ends the project on the spot, while a green fake door merely lets it continue.

## Sequencing rules that hold up

Desk work before human contact. Wedge cut plus teardown cost 5 hours combined and require no outreach, and they sharpen every question you will ask in interviews.
Money signal before traffic signal. Run the pricing probe before the channel test. Traffic to an offer nobody will pay for is a vanity number, and an 8 hour channel test on an unpriced offer measures curiosity, not demand.
Gate the channel test on demand evidence: do not spend the 8 hours until the fake door converts above roughly 3 percent of unique visitors, or you have collected at least five unprompted "when can I buy this" replies.
One test at a time. Parallel tests contaminate each other's evidence: when a pricing probe and a fake door run together, you cannot tell whether prospects balked at the price or at the promise.

## When to stop testing and build

Three stop conditions, and any one is enough. First, the next cheapest remaining test costs more than building the wedge itself: at that point shipping is the cheaper experiment. Second, you hold two independent positive signals from different dimensions. Severity plus willingness to pay is the strongest pair, because it means the pain is real and already priced. Third, tests keep returning weak positives: ship anyway, because live activation and 90 day churn settle what interviews and probes cannot. Hard cap the whole sequence at 26 hours of validation work. Past that budget you are no longer validating, you are hiding from a verdict. Ship it is listed at 0 hours because the wedge build replaces the lab: real signups, real churn, real revenue, the only evidence that never lies about demand.
