# Past Behavior Beats Hypothetical Praise

## Future-tense answers score zero

Any statement about what someone would do is a polite guess, not evidence. "I would totally pay for this" costs the speaker nothing, commits them to nothing, and predicts almost nothing about willingness to pay. People agree to hypothetical purchases at several times the rate they actually buy, because agreeing is the socially cheap move. The heuristic: when grounding a problem severity score, weight any future-tense or conditional statement at zero. Filter for the words "would," "could," "probably," "definitely," and "once you add." Every one of them marks an opinion about an imagined future, and imagined futures are where weak ideas go to feel validated.

## Recurring spend is the strongest severity signal

"We currently pay an intern to do this every Friday" is gold because it packs three verifiable facts into one sentence: the problem recurs on a fixed schedule, the company already allocates budget to it, and a named person owns the task. That is proof of severity and a willingness-to-pay anchor in the same breath. Rank evidence in this order: money spent on a recurring basis, then a built workaround such as a spreadsheet, script, or automation chain, then quantified recurring time with a number attached, then one specific story about the last occurrence, then unquantified complaining. If the best available evidence never rises above complaint, cap the severity score low no matter how loud the complaining gets. A budget line also lowers churn risk: replacing a paid workaround is stickier than replacing enthusiasm.

## Compliment-noise patterns in public threads

Public threads on forums and launch sites are dominated by applause: "great idea," "I need this," "take my money," and upvotes with no story attached. Applause is a channel signal at best, never a severity signal. The filter: a comment counts as behavioral evidence only when it contains a first-person, past-tense verb plus at least one specific detail, meaning a tool name, a dollar figure, a frequency, or a job title. "I need this" is worth zero. "Our ops lead updates a Google Sheet every Monday because our CRM cannot export this view" is worth a lot. Expect roughly 1 in 5 comments in a genuinely painful problem thread to carry a behavioral fact. A thread that is pure agreement tells you where the audience gathers, which helps your channel and wedge, but it says nothing about how badly the problem hurts.

## Vocabulary that marks a behavioral fact

Mine threads and interview notes for four word families. Frequency words: every Friday, each sprint, at month-end close. Money words: we pay, subscription, invoice, chargeback, we got refunded. Workaround words: manually, export to CSV, duct-taped, we wrote a script, someone copies it over. Consequence words: missed the deadline, lost the client, got fined, shipped late. Each family maps directly to a severity component: recurrence, existing spend, effort cost, and downside cost. One dated, specific, first-person account of the last time the problem occurred outweighs 50 generic upvotes, because it can be checked and the upvotes cannot.

## Scoring rubric for severity evidence

Translate evidence quality into score ceilings. Documented recurring spend, a paid tool, a contractor, or an intern on a schedule, supports severity scores above 70. A built workaround plus a quantified time cost supports 50 to 70. Specific past stories without any cost figure support 30 to 50. Pure future-tense enthusiasm caps at 20 regardless of volume, even with a thousand upvotes behind it. When severity evidence is thin, the correct next step is not a landing page test, it is five problem interviews recruited from the exact community where the complaints surfaced, structured to ask only about the past: what happened last time, what it cost, and what they did about it.
