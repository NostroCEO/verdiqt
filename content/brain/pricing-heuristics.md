# Pricing Heuristics

## Charge Along the Value Metric, Not the Convenient One

Heuristic: pick the unit that grows when the customer wins, and put your price on that unit. A value metric passes three tests. Usage of it correlates with the outcome the buyer pays for, the buyer can predict their own number before signup, and they cannot game it without losing the benefit. Seats, API calls, records, campaigns sent, gigabytes processed: each is only correct when more of it means more delivered value for that specific buyer. Evidence pattern to watch during validation: if heavy users and light users would pay the same price under your metric, willingness to pay is decoupled from the metric and expansion revenue will stall. A wrong metric also shows up as churn concentrated in accounts whose bill grew while their outcome did not. When scoring an idea, treat a clean, obvious value metric as a monetization strength and a fuzzy one as a structural weakness that no pricing page can fix.

## Anchor the Price to the Cost of the Problem

Heuristic: price is justified by the pain removed, never by the effort to build. Quantify the problem in one of three currencies before setting a number. Hours lost: multiply weekly hours burned by a loaded hourly rate, then charge 10 to 30 percent of that annual figure. Revenue missed: estimate deals, leads, or renewals lost to the problem, then charge a single-digit percentage of recovered revenue. Tools replaced: sum the line items your product retires and price at or below that total, since displacement budgets already exist and require no new approval. Severity drives the ceiling: a problem the buyer already measures in money supports a price ten times higher than one they merely find annoying. Evidence pattern: in interviews, buyers who state the cost of the problem unprompted, in numbers, are high willingness to pay. Buyers who need you to explain why the problem matters will churn at any price.

## When Usage Pricing Beats Seats

Heuristic: charge per usage when value concentrates in a few power users, and per seat when value spreads through collaboration. Seat pricing works when each added person independently gets the outcome, which is why it fits communication and workflow tools. It fails when one operator serves the whole company, because the account never expands past two or three seats regardless of delivered value. Usage pricing wins in that case, and also when consumption is spiky, when the buyer is an API or automation rather than a human, or when your wedge is a small technical team inside a large company that will grow spend through a self-serve channel. Observable signals: seat counts flat while usage grows 5x means switch to usage. Bills that swing more than 3x month to month scare buyers, so add committed tiers with overage. Hybrid models, a platform fee plus metered usage, protect revenue floor while capturing expansion.

## Set a Floor That Filters Wrong-Fit Customers

Heuristic: your minimum price is a qualification filter, not just revenue. A floor near 50 to 100 dollars per month screens out hobbyists whose churn is structural, whose support load is highest, and whose feedback drags the roadmap toward free-tier features. If target buyers balk at a modest floor, the finding is diagnostic: either severity is too low or you are talking to the wrong segment, and the fix is repositioning, not discounting. Evidence pattern: cheap plans showing 8 to 15 percent monthly churn next to premium plans at 2 to 3 percent means the floor is too low and the low tier is subsidized noise. Raise the floor until sales conversations start with the problem instead of the price.
