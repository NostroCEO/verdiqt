# Painkiller vs Vitamin: Severity Tests Before You Build

A painkiller gets bought this quarter. A vitamin gets bookmarked, praised in interviews, and never expensed. Problem severity is observable before a single line of code exists, and it predicts willingness to pay and long-term churn better than any survey answer. Four evidence classes separate the two.

## Urgency Markers in Complaint Language

Read raw complaints from forums, reviews, support threads, and community posts, and score the language itself. Vitamin language is conditional and polite: "it would be nice if," "someday we should," "has anyone found a better way." Painkiller language carries three markers: a deadline, a number, and a consequence. "We missed payroll cutoff twice this month," "I spend six hours every Friday rebuilding this report," "we lost a client over this." Present tense and recurring frequency beat hypothetical framing every time. Working heuristic: if fewer than 1 in 5 complaints about the problem names a quantified cost in hours, dollars, or lost deals, severity is low and the idea is a vitamin regardless of how many people mention it.

## Workaround-Building Is Proof of Pain

Nobody builds a workaround for a mild annoyance. A duct-tape spreadsheet with formulas and tabs, a chained automation across three tools, a shared doc with a manual checklist run every Monday: each one is a prototype the market already built, and it marks a real budget of time. Grade workarounds by maintenance cost, not cleverness. A spreadsheet touched daily by two people signals stronger severity than an elegant script run once a quarter. Working heuristic: a workaround that consumes 2 or more hours per week, per person, is painkiller evidence, and the workaround itself defines your wedge, because replacing something that exists is easier than creating a habit that does not. An abandoned workaround is the opposite signal: the pain was not worth the upkeep.

## Budget-Line Evidence

Severe problems already have money assigned to them somewhere. Ask where the cost of the problem lives today: a salary line, part of an assistant's job description, an agency retainer, an overtime bill, a penalty fee paid to a vendor. If a buyer can point to the spend, your product displaces an existing budget line, which means a shorter sales cycle and lower churn when budgets tighten. If nobody can locate the spend, you are asking the buyer to create a new budget line, the hardest sale in SaaS and the first line cut in a downturn. Working heuristic: existing spend to displace scores as painkiller; a net-new budget line scores as vitamin unless the pain evidence elsewhere is overwhelming, and it usually forces an education-heavy channel with worse unit economics.

## Willingness-to-Pay Proxies

Stated pricing intent is nearly worthless. Paid behavior is the proxy that holds up: the target already pays for adjacent tools in the same workflow, hires humans (a VA, a freelancer, an agency) to do the task manually, or maintains internal tooling an engineer must service. Each proxy also anchors price. If an account pays a contractor 2,000 a month to do the job by hand, a 200-per-month tool is a defensible entry point with a 10x cost advantage. Working heuristic: score willingness to pay as proven only when you can name the current paid substitute and its monthly cost; then combine the four evidence classes into a 0-4 severity score, one point each for urgent complaint language, an active workaround, a named budget line, and a paid substitute. A score of 3 or higher is a painkiller worth pursuing; at 2 or below, expect trial users who compliment the product and churn at renewal.
